A yes on the phone, a surprise at billing

About Valmo

Valmo is Meesho's logistics network. It hires trucks from independent transporters on monthly route contracts, then pays them per completed trip.

What I designed

Contracts reached transporters as messages and calls. A yes had no record, and a wrong rate only surfaced at billing. I designed the Contract Management panel, the transporter's side of Valmo's contract system. Every contract can be accepted, rejected, disputed or ended on screen, with the money shown before each decision. Whatever he decides lands on the Valmo team's desk as named work, and whatever they change shows up on his row.

Contracts were agreed
where nobody could check

A contract is a promise first and trips later: one monthly contract turns into roughly twenty runs, each paid on completion. Two groups depended on that contract being right.

Transporters
Run the trucks and take 15 to 70 contracts a month, most of them in one monthly batch. They decide between trips, in minutes, and want the cost of a contract clear before they run it.
Valmo linehaul team
Assign each route to a transporter at a rate, chase every answer and resolve disputes before a contract's start date.
Agreeing by phone broke down in two places.
A yes on a call is not a record

Acceptance happened over chat or a phone call. When a rate was questioned weeks later, neither side could show what had been agreed.

A wrong rate surfaced at billing

A rate he never agreed to stayed invisible until the payout came in short, long after the trips had run.

Nobody disagreed on purpose. The rate was just never written down where both sides could see it.

Accepting, questioning or ending
a contract took a call

Every contract question, from whether a deadline had passed to why a rate was lower, went to whoever answered at Valmo. The answer depended on who was on shift.

Transporters asked what saying no would cost

In conversations with transporters, the same three questions kept coming up: which contracts need me now, which are running, and which are over. Their problem was never too few contracts. It was not knowing which one needed a decision and not knowing what saying no would cost.

What the panel had to respect

01
Contracts arrive in one batch
Most of a month's contracts land on the same day, so “new” carries no signal. Urgency had to come from the deadline.
02
Two languages, one screen
Transporters decide in Hindi. Every dispute reason he picks carries a Hindi line underneath.
03
Ending a contract is irreversible
Terminating gives up the rest of a contract worth a lakh or more. The panel had to show that before the click.
04
Trips keep running during a dispute
A rate question can't stop the trucks. A dispute had to run alongside the trips, not block them.

Show the money before every decision

He decides in minutes, between trips. So every screen assumes short attention, plain words and one obvious action. From what transporters and the ops team told me, I set four rules:

01
One contract, one stage. Pending, Upcoming, Active or Closed, never two.
02
Named views, not filters. Under Dispute and Ending Early are saved questions with counts, and All is always there.
03
One primary action per row. Accept is the only button on a pending row; Reject is a text link under it.
04
The panel does the math. Earnings and potential loss are computed and shown, so he never has to derive a number.

Five moments where
the panel shows the stakes

1. Every pending row says its own deadline

Problem
Contracts arrive in one batch, so on batch day every row looked new and the deadline that mattered was buried.
Ruled out
I ruled out sorting by arrival or a “New” label.
Decision
Each row writes its deadline first, “Accept by 3hrs” or “Accept by Tomorrow”, and a chip says when the system last nudged him. Three cards on top count what is pending and what it is worth, what is disputed and what is ending, and each says what changed.
Key detail
Accepting is a review, not a click. One sheet shows the whole deal: route, period, placement time, vehicle size and rate per trip, with the key terms above a checkbox Accept Contract waits for. From a reminder, it takes him back to the rest, “Back to reminders (2 more left)”.

2. Saying no comes with a number

Problem
Rejecting a contract ended the deal. A rate he couldn't accept was simply lost.
Ruled out
I ruled out a plain reason list that treats every refusal the same.
Decision
Rejection is allowed, and it is priced. The potential earning loss, total and per trip, sits above the reasons. Choosing “Rate not acceptable” asks for his rate, which goes to Valmo as a counter-offer.
Key detail
The reason reaches the Valmo manager reassigning the route in his own words, and Go Back sits beside Confirm, so backing out is one click at every step.

3. A counter-offer lands as a new contract

Problem
When Valmo answered a rate question, there was nowhere for the new number to land.
Ruled out
I ruled out quietly rewriting the old row's rate.
Decision
One card shows three rates, old, requested and Valmo's, with Valmo's note. Accepting issues a new contract and links the two.
Key detail
On the list, the old rate is struck through beside the new one and marked Updated, so a resolved dispute changes the number in daylight. If he doesn't accept, his current rate stays and the trips keep running.

4. A dispute is built, not written

Problem
A dispute was a paragraph in an email, with no category, owner or status.
Ruled out
I ruled out free text and a language switch for the whole panel.
Decision
Reasons are picked, not typed, each with a Hindi line, and each asks for exactly the data that resolves it: a rate asks what he expected, a vehicle size asks what he can provide, a period asks what he can serve.
Key detail
The form promises up front that trips keep running and settlement is adjusted once resolved. The row then carries the dispute's status, from under review to approved, partly approved or rejected.

5. Ending a contract names who ended it

Problem
A contract that stopped early just disappeared, and “Closed” didn't say who closed it.
Ruled out
I ruled out one generic Closed status.
Decision
Ending a live contract is deliberately heavy. Terminate shows the earning loss and the last active date first, still asks for a reason, and uses the same reason list as rejecting and disputing, so Valmo reads one language. Closed rows say who ended it: “Ended early by You”, “Ended early by Valmo”, “Cancelled by Valmo”.
Key detail
Completed is earned: it appears only on contracts that ran their full period. “Continued as CR-…” links an old contract to the one that replaced it.

Empty tabs, missed trips
and wrong IDs, handled

Most of the calls came from moments when the panel had nothing to show or something had gone wrong. I designed each of those states on purpose.

6. Nothing is left blank

  • Empty tabs guide, not stall
    Every tab has its own empty state. An empty Pending tab says “All caught up” instead of showing a blank table.
  • Missed trips are named
    On a running contract, a missed trip reads “Missed · Placement failure” in the trip progress, not a gap in the count.
  • A wrong ID fails in place
    Typing a contract ID that doesn't exist says “No contract found” right under the field, before he fills in anything else.

7. Contract and trip disputes, side by side

A rate question about the whole contract and a problem with one trip are raised from the same place, and the form explains how the two coexist.

8. Every answer lands on someone's desk

The panel is one half of a loop. When he accepts, the contract moves to Valmo's Upcoming. A rejection lands with its reason, a dispute opens with a five-day clock, and an early ending starts a countdown. When Valmo revises a rate, his row shows the new number, marked Updated.

9. Getting in doesn't need a call either

Contracts are money, so the section carries its own lock, written for a first-time user. First login sets a password, a forgotten one is reset with an OTP on his own phone, and a support address sits on the gate for anyone stuck at the first step.

Where the panel
still falls short

  • Counts must come from one source
    The overview said 10 pending while the tab said 30. To a literal reader, a mismatch reads as the system lying. I would tie every count to one query.
  • One reason list has a cost
    Reject, dispute and Terminate share one list, so he learns it once and Valmo reads one language. But ending a running contract has reasons that rejecting doesn't. I would add those without breaking the shared list.
  • Put what he's judged on where he works
    Performance decides his next contracts, but it lives one page deep. I would bring it onto the list.
